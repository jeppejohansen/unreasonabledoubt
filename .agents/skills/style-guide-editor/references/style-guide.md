# Unreasonable Doubt — Voice and Style Guide

This guide draws on the two published posts in `published_posts/`. Their extracted text contains PDF and web-page artifacts; use the posts for their reasoning and voice, not those artifacts.

## Shape of an argument

- Open with a concrete puzzle or two observations that seem to conflict. Say why the question matters and what the post will explain.
- Introduce a deliberately simple model soon after the puzzle. State its assumptions and define its terms before drawing conclusions.
- Work through at least one concrete case. The dating post uses named people and numbers; the housing post traces a housing expansion through its graphs.
- Derive the mechanism step by step. When using a figure, tell the reader what its axes and colors mean, where to start, what moves, and what follows.
- State the conditions under which the result holds. Separate a model result from an empirical claim or a forecast about the world.
- Close by answering the opening question and naming the assumption or parameter that could change the answer.

This is a useful sequence, not a required set of section headings.

## Voice and paragraphs

- Write for a curious general reader. A conversational first person can explain why a puzzle matters, while the model and evidence carry the claim.
- Use direct claims and plain definitions for jargon and symbols. Explain equations in words, including what each parameter changes.
- Give each paragraph one job: pose a claim, explain a step, show evidence, or qualify an implication. Split paragraphs that repeat the same point or change jobs midway.
- Use informative headings and signposts such as “Concretely,” “Intuitively,” or “Put differently” when they clarify a transition. Avoid repeating them by habit.
- Use rhetorical questions sparingly. Follow a question with a direct answer rather than several more questions.
- Use bullets for steps or assumptions when they make a mechanism easier to follow.

## Evidence and sourcing

- Cite empirical facts and outside claims near the claim they support, preferably with an authoritative source.
- Label assumptions, simulated outcomes, and observed facts clearly. Do not present a model's conditional result as a measured effect.
- Describe what a figure shows before interpreting its implications.
